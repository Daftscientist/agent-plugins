# Compositions

A catalogue of slide-structure archetypes for the BUILD stage. Each entry states when to use it, its structure, where visual weight sits, what it's good for, and its common failure mode. These are structural patterns, not coordinates — build each one for the actual content in front of you; see `references/authoring-html.md` for HTML/CSS technique.

### Statement
**When:** one sentence is the entire point of the slide.
**Structure:** large centered or left-anchored text, minimal else.
**Weight:** the sentence itself.
**Good for:** pivotal claims, section transitions, the moment before an ask.
**Failure:** adding a supporting graphic that dilutes the sentence's isolation.

### Hero
**When:** opening a deck or a major section with impact.
**Structure:** large display text (and optionally a full-bleed image) filling most of the frame.
**Weight:** the headline.
**Good for:** covers, keynote openers, section starts.
**Failure:** cramming a subtitle, logo, date, and tagline all at equal size.

### Title / cover
**When:** the first slide of the deck.
**Structure:** deck title, short subtitle, presenter/date metadata kept small.
**Weight:** the title.
**Good for:** every deck.
**Failure:** treating the cover as a dumping ground for a logo wall.

### Section divider
**When:** marking a new part of a multi-part deck.
**Structure:** section number/label, short section title, sparse.
**Weight:** the section label.
**Good for:** long decks that need wayfinding.
**Failure:** dividers that look identical to content slides, giving no sense of structure.

### Split 50/50
**When:** two elements of genuinely equal importance.
**Structure:** two equal columns/halves.
**Weight:** balanced across both sides.
**Good for:** content + image, argument + evidence, before/after.
**Failure:** using it when one side is clearly more important — forced balance flattens hierarchy.

### Split 40/60
**When:** two elements where one is primary.
**Structure:** a smaller supporting column beside a larger primary column.
**Weight:** the 60% side.
**Good for:** a main point with a supporting chart/quote/image.
**Failure:** making the 40% side visually louder than the 60% side by accident.

### Editorial asymmetric
**When:** open design freedom and a strong single idea.
**Structure:** off-grid placement, generous whitespace, one dominant element.
**Weight:** deliberately uneven, pulled to one zone.
**Good for:** editorial/brand-forward decks, differentiated moments in an otherwise systematic deck.
**Failure:** using it on every slide, which erases its distinctiveness and just looks inconsistent.

### Image-led
**When:** the image itself carries the argument.
**Structure:** large or full-bleed image with minimal overlay text.
**Weight:** the image.
**Good for:** product shots, documentary evidence, mood-setting.
**Failure:** low-quality or irrelevant stock imagery used because a slide "needed a picture."

### Text-led
**When:** the argument is verbal/conceptual, not visual.
**Structure:** structured text hierarchy with little or no imagery.
**Weight:** typography itself.
**Good for:** dense arguments, legal/technical points, quotes.
**Failure:** wall-of-text with no hierarchy inside it.

### Single metric
**When:** one number carries the argument.
**Structure:** hero number, short qualifier, one contextual comparison/evidence line.
**Weight:** the number dominates.
**Good for:** a headline KPI, the one number a reader must remember.
**Failure:** converting it into three equal KPI cards and losing the point.

### Metric + evidence
**When:** a number needs one piece of supporting proof.
**Structure:** metric on one side, a small chart/quote/comparison on the other.
**Weight:** metric primary, evidence secondary.
**Good for:** traction slides, results slides.
**Failure:** evidence competing visually with the metric instead of supporting it.

### Three metrics
**When:** three genuinely co-equal numbers.
**Structure:** three columns, identical treatment.
**Weight:** distributed evenly across all three.
**Good for:** a balanced scorecard moment.
**Failure:** forcing a fourth or second metric to "balance" the layout instead of the content.

### Comparison
**When:** two options/states need to be weighed against each other.
**Structure:** two aligned columns/rows with matched fields.
**Weight:** balanced, with the differentiator emphasized.
**Good for:** us-vs-competitor, plan tiers, before/after.
**Failure:** unmatched fields between the two sides, making comparison actually impossible.

### Before/after
**When:** showing change over an intervention.
**Structure:** before state and after state, same framing, side by side or stacked.
**Weight:** the after state, usually.
**Good for:** transformation narratives, case studies.
**Failure:** different scales/framing between the two states, which misleads rather than clarifies.

### Two-column argument
**When:** two supporting points for one conclusion.
**Structure:** two parallel content blocks under one shared headline.
**Weight:** the shared headline, then evenly split below it.
**Good for:** cause/effect, problem/opportunity pairs.
**Failure:** the two columns not actually being parallel in structure or depth.

### Three-part argument
**When:** three supporting points for one conclusion.
**Structure:** three parallel blocks under a shared headline.
**Weight:** the headline first, then evenly split.
**Good for:** pillars, principles, phases.
**Failure:** padding to exactly three when the real content is two or four.

### Grid
**When:** several genuinely comparable items.
**Structure:** uniform rows/columns of equal-weight cells.
**Weight:** even across all cells.
**Good for:** feature lists, logo walls, portfolio thumbnails.
**Failure:** using a grid for items that aren't actually equal in importance.

### Bento
**When:** several related but differently-weighted items.
**Structure:** irregular grid of variously sized cards.
**Weight:** distributed by card size.
**Good for:** product feature overviews, dashboards-as-slide.
**Failure:** the default reach for "several things to show" regardless of whether the items actually differ in weight — overused, it becomes the same tell as the identical-cards failure.

### Process
**When:** a sequence of discrete steps.
**Structure:** steps in a row or column, connected by a line/arrow.
**Weight:** even across steps, or emphasized on the current/relevant step.
**Good for:** how something works, onboarding flow.
**Failure:** more than ~6 steps crammed into one row, unreadable at scale.

### Timeline
**When:** events across real time.
**Structure:** a horizontal or vertical axis with dated markers.
**Weight:** the axis itself plus emphasized milestone(s).
**Good for:** company history, project plans.
**Failure:** uneven time compression that visually implies a false pace.

### Roadmap
**When:** planned future work across time or phases.
**Structure:** phases/quarters as columns or a horizontal track, items placed within.
**Weight:** current/near-term phase emphasized.
**Good for:** product/company planning decks.
**Failure:** implying false certainty on far-future items with the same visual confidence as near-term ones.

### Funnel
**When:** a narrowing sequence (volume decreasing at each stage).
**Structure:** stacked trapezoids or bars narrowing top to bottom.
**Weight:** proportional to actual volume at each stage.
**Good for:** conversion, sales pipeline, marketing funnel.
**Failure:** decorative funnel shape with stages not actually proportional to real numbers.

### Flywheel
**When:** a reinforcing cyclical system.
**Structure:** circular arrangement with directional arrows.
**Weight:** even around the cycle, or emphasized at the reinforcing loop.
**Good for:** growth loops, network-effect businesses.
**Failure:** drawing a flywheel for a process that is actually linear, not cyclical.

### Matrix
**When:** items classified along two independent axes.
**Structure:** 2D grid with labeled axes.
**Weight:** even across cells, emphasis on the occupied/relevant cell(s).
**Good for:** positioning, prioritization frameworks.
**Failure:** axes that aren't actually independent, producing a matrix that doesn't mean anything.

### Quadrant
**When:** a specific four-way strategic framing (e.g. impact vs. effort).
**Structure:** four-cell grid with a plotted position or items placed per cell.
**Weight:** the quadrant containing the key point.
**Good for:** strategic positioning, prioritization.
**Failure:** plotting without real justification for placement — looks precise, isn't.

### Stack / layers
**When:** showing a layered system (e.g. tech stack).
**Structure:** horizontal bands stacked vertically, each a layer.
**Weight:** even, or emphasized on the layer being discussed.
**Good for:** architecture, product stack, org layers.
**Failure:** layers that don't actually depend on each other in the order shown.

### Architecture / system
**When:** explaining how technical or organizational components connect.
**Structure:** boxes/nodes with labeled connections, clear boundaries.
**Weight:** the component under discussion, or the data-flow path.
**Good for:** technical decks, system explanations.
**Failure:** every box drawn with equal visual loudness, so nothing reads as the important path. See `references/diagrams.md`.

### Ecosystem
**When:** showing multiple independent participants around a hub.
**Structure:** central hub with surrounding participant nodes.
**Weight:** the hub, or a highlighted participant.
**Good for:** platform/marketplace positioning.
**Failure:** so many nodes that none of them can be individually read.

### Map / spatial
**When:** content has real geographic or spatial meaning.
**Structure:** a map or floorplan with data overlaid.
**Weight:** the highlighted region(s).
**Good for:** geographic expansion, regional data, physical layout.
**Failure:** using a map decoratively when the geography isn't actually relevant to the point.

### Table
**When:** exact values and cross-dimensional comparison matter.
**Structure:** rows/columns with a clear header hierarchy.
**Weight:** the row/column being emphasized.
**Good for:** pricing, detailed comparisons, reference data. See `references/tables.md`.
**Failure:** a table used when a chart or single metric would communicate the point faster.

### Chart + takeaway
**When:** a chart exists to prove a specific conclusion.
**Structure:** chart plus a stated conclusion, usually as the slide title.
**Weight:** the takeaway text and the highlighted series together.
**Good for:** any data-backed claim. See `references/data-design.md`.
**Failure:** a chart with a topic-label title ("Revenue") instead of the conclusion it proves.

### Chart + annotations
**When:** specific points on a chart need explanation.
**Structure:** chart with callouts/annotations at key data points.
**Weight:** the annotated point(s), not the whole series equally.
**Good for:** explaining an inflection, an anomaly, an event's impact.
**Failure:** annotating every point, which defeats the purpose of annotation as emphasis.

### Quote
**When:** a third-party statement carries more weight than a paraphrase would.
**Structure:** large quote text, attribution kept small.
**Weight:** the quote itself.
**Good for:** customer proof, testimonials, notable endorsements.
**Failure:** a quote so generic it could apply to any product.

### Case study
**When:** one customer/example proves the point in depth.
**Structure:** context, action, result — usually three beats on one or two slides.
**Weight:** the result.
**Good for:** proof-heavy decks, sales decks.
**Failure:** so much context that the actual result gets buried at the bottom.

### Customer proof
**When:** breadth of validation matters more than depth of one story.
**Structure:** logos, short quotes, or metrics from multiple customers.
**Weight:** even across customers, unless one is materially more credible.
**Failure:** a logo wall with no differentiation and no indication of what it actually proves.

### Team
**When:** who's involved matters to the audience's decision.
**Structure:** individual photos/names/roles with the credential relevant to this specific pitch.
**Weight:** the credential most relevant to the audience's concern, not a full CV.
**Good for:** pitch decks, proposals.
**Failure:** generic bios with no connection to why this team can execute this specific plan.

### Portfolio / gallery
**When:** showing a body of work or a set of examples.
**Structure:** grid or filmstrip of thumbnails.
**Weight:** even, or emphasized on the strongest example.
**Good for:** creative/agency decks, case-study overviews.
**Failure:** inconsistent thumbnail crops/aspect ratios that look sloppy side by side.

### Product screenshot
**When:** the actual product UI is the evidence.
**Structure:** framed or floating screenshot, cropped to the relevant region.
**Weight:** the screenshot.
**Good for:** product demos, feature proof.
**Failure:** a full, unedited screenshot with irrelevant chrome, too small to read the part that matters. See `references/imagery.md`.

### Feature walkthrough
**When:** stepping through several product capabilities.
**Structure:** repeated screenshot + short explanation pattern, one feature per slide or per row.
**Weight:** the screenshot per step.
**Good for:** product-led decks, onboarding decks.
**Failure:** identical framing repeated so mechanically it stops reading as content and starts reading as template filler.

### Closing / ask
**When:** the final slide before Q&A or a decision.
**Structure:** restated core claim plus the specific ask/next step.
**Weight:** the ask.
**Good for:** every deck that wants something from its audience.
**Failure:** ending on a generic "Thank you" with no ask restated.

### Appendix / reference
**When:** supporting detail that shouldn't clutter the main narrative.
**Structure:** denser, more table/reference-like than the main deck.
**Weight:** none — density is acceptable here in a way it isn't in the main narrative.
**Good for:** backup data, detailed methodology, definitions.
**Failure:** appendix content that actually belongs in the main narrative, hidden where it won't be seen.
